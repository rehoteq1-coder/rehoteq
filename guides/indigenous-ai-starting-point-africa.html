The Starting Point and the Time: Building African AI With Open Tools

Where African-built AI actually begins: language and data, the open community already doing the work, and a four-step starting point.

By Rehoteq Editorial TeamLast updated: 28 October 2026
About the author
Rehoteq Editorial Team produces practical explainers for Nigerian households, schools and small organisations. Read our editorial policy.

Language is the starting point

Ask where African artificial intelligence should begin and most answers point at hardware, funding or government policy. All three matter. The true starting point is smaller and closer: language. Roughly two thousand of the world’s languages are African, about a third of all human speech, and very few of them exist inside today’s AI systems in usable form. A machine that has never read Yoruba proverbs, heard Twi spoken in a market, or seen an African name spelled correctly cannot serve the people inside that world. It can only translate them into somebody else’s.

That starting point also answers the timing question. The moment to build is when building no longer requires permission from the large platforms, and open tools have moved us there. Small language models tuned to a handful of languages now run on hardware a small team can afford, and the bottleneck has shifted from compute to conviction: someone has to record the elders, type the corpus and ship the tool.

The work has already started, and it is public

This is not a proposal waiting for a benefactor. In 2019 researchers Jade Abbott and Vukosi Marivate started Masakhane, a grassroots natural language processing community whose isiZulu name means “we build together.” Volunteers across the continent have produced translation models and datasets covering dozens of African languages, and the MasakhaNER named-entity work is open on GitHub for anyone to build on. In Ghana, the GhanaNLP collective turned volunteer research into Khaya, a translation app for Ghanaian languages with thousands of users. In Kenya, the KenCorpus project’s community-built datasets have been downloaded more than half a million times. In South Africa, Lelapa AI released Vulavula, a voice-to-text tool whose name means “speak” in Xitsonga, and later InkubaLM, one of Africa’s first multilingual small language models for low-resource languages. The Deep Learning Indaba and its country chapters keep feeding the pipeline with trained researchers.

None of these teams waited for perfect conditions. They started with researchers, open-source tools and languages their own families speak.

A four-step starting point

  1. Collect. Record and transcribe real speech, label real text, and keep licensing clean so the data stays usable and African-owned.
  2. Learn in public. The open baselines from Masakhane and its peers are a working curriculum; read the repos, reproduce a result, then improve it.
  3. Build something narrow. A translation app, a voice note-taker for traders, a homework helper in the mother tongue beats a grand platform with no users.
  4. Keep the base open. A shared, well-maintained base model beats ten closed ones nobody can afford to run.
Limits of this guide: it is strategy, not a technical implementation plan. Dataset collection carries consent, privacy and licensing obligations that vary by community and jurisdiction. Project details (team names, product status, download counts) were verified against published reporting in 2026 and can change; check each project’s own pages before building on it.

The starting point is language and data. The time is now, because the tools finally reward small, deliberate teams. What is still missing in most places is the decision to begin, and that is the one input no investor supplies.
