Sizing a Solar System for a Nigerian Home

A worked path from an essential-load list to battery capacity, inverter ratings and a first panel-energy estimate.

Rehoteq Editorial Team · Updated 23 September 2026 · Editorial policy

How to read this lesson. The worked scenarios are original, illustrative training examples, not documented customer jobs or claims of a completed inspection. No named practitioner review is claimed. Use the sources and ask a competent professional to check decisions for your situation.

“How many panels do I need for a 5 kW inverter?” sounds precise, but it starts at the wrong end. The inverter tells you something about power at a moment. It does not tell you how long the family runs the fans, whether a freezer stays on overnight, or how much energy the roof can recover during a wet week. Start with the service you need during an outage, then make each sizing assumption visible.

This walkthrough is for a household preparing an installer brief, not someone preparing to connect equipment. Its deliberately small example keeps cooking, water heating, pumping and air conditioning outside the backup supply. If those loads matter to you, list them explicitly. Do not quietly add them after agreeing a design.

1. Write the load list first

Use the load-audit guide to record appliance quantity, electrical input watts, usage hours and whether it must run during an outage. Distinguish a measured average from a nameplate maximum. For a cycling refrigerator, one instantaneous reading does not describe a full day. A competent technician can measure representative energy without asking the owner to open a panel.

Our example is an evening essentials package. Six lamps rated 10 W run for five hours: 6 × 10 × 5 = 300 Wh. Two fans rated 60 W run for six hours: 720 Wh. One 80 W television runs for four hours: 320 Wh. A 12 W router runs for eight hours: 96 Wh. The total is 1,436 Wh, or 1.436 kWh. With every listed appliance on, running power is 60 + 120 + 80 + 12 = 272 W.

Those are different totals answering different questions. The 1.436 kWh figure is the energy to deliver across the chosen hours; 272 W is the simultaneous running load. A phone charger added for an hour changes energy slightly, while a pump starting briefly may dominate the inverter requirement. Record both instead of hiding everything inside an “average house” allowance.

2. Size usable storage, then nominal storage

Assume the entire example load energy comes from the battery, inverter efficiency is 90%, and the chosen planning depth of discharge is 80%. Add a 20% energy reserve for this exercise. Nominal battery energy = 1,436 × 1.20 ÷ (0.90 × 0.80) = 2,393 Wh, approximately 2.39 kWh. At a nominal bank voltage of 48 V, that is 2,393 ÷ 48 = 49.86 Ah for the bank.

Using 51.2 V instead produces about 46.74 Ah for the same energy. That difference is why comparing batteries by Ah alone is misleading. Quote nominal voltage, nominal kWh, usable kWh, allowed discharge current and warranty conditions together. Our battery and inverter calculator reproduces this exact example; change one assumption at a time to understand what matters.

Now change only the planning DoD to 50%, as a conservative lead-acid example. The requirement becomes 1,436 × 1.20 ÷ (0.90 × 0.50) = 3,829 Wh, or 79.78 Ah at 48 V. This is still an energy estimate, not a battery selection. Lead-acid capacity varies with discharge rate, temperature, condition and charging history. Lithium batteries have their own temperature and BMS constraints. The battery guide explains the comparison questions.

3. Check W, VA and starting demand

For this example, a 25% running-power margin gives 272 × 1.25 = 340 W. At an assumed aggregate power factor of 0.8, the apparent-power target is 340 ÷ 0.8 = 425 VA. Both are continuous-rating checks. Neither means a particular small inverter is suitable: waveform, thermal derating, motor starts, charger features and battery compatibility remain to be checked.

A manufacturer may state a surge rating that lasts seconds, while the appliance needs a different current profile. “Double the watts” is not a reliable universal starting rule. Give the installer the appliance model, not just the category “freezer.” Also ask what happens when low battery voltage coincides with a restart after an outage.

At 272 W, 48 V and 90% efficiency, approximate running battery current is 272 ÷ (48 × 0.9) = 6.30 A. That does not size a fuse or DC cable. Starting current, lower operating voltage and fault energy require separate checks. A battery with enough kWh can still have an inadequate BMS current limit.

4. Make the panel-energy assumption explicit

For a first energy exercise, suppose the chosen site and design month provide four equivalent peak-sun-hours daily, and use an overall PV-to-delivered-load factor of 0.70. These are illustrative assumptions, not a measured Nigerian national yield. Daily panel power for the reserved load energy is 1.436 × 1.20 ÷ (4 × 0.70) = 0.615 kWp. Do not apply the battery efficiency loss again if it is already included in the overall 0.70 factor.

Two hypothetical 400 W modules provide 0.8 kWp of nameplate capacity. That exceeds 0.615 kWp on paper, but does not establish a valid array. The installer still checks minimum and maximum MPPT voltage, cold open-circuit voltage, current limits, string arrangement, shade, roof area, structure and protection. There is no permission here to connect two modules in series or parallel.

Cloudy-day resilience is another design decision. Recharging a depleted battery while serving daytime loads may require more array power and a suitable charging window. A dry-season average can hide a wet-season shortfall. Ask for site-specific yield assumptions and a bad-weather operating plan, not merely a promise that “the panels will charge it.”

5. The on-site assessment and handover

A useful site brief contains the load sheet, essential circuits, expected outage duration, space for batteries, roof photographs taken safely from ground level, and the household’s future-load plans. The professional survey should confirm shading, mounting suitability, cable routes, ambient conditions, earthing, protection and separation from existing generator or grid supplies. These are checks to request, not tasks to perform yourself.

Require a proposal that ties each piece of equipment to a reason. Ask for model numbers, a compatibility statement, the excluded loads, commissioning records and an explanation of shutdown and fault reporting. “5 kVA package” without battery energy, PV limits or operating assumptions is not a complete description. Use the solar cost tool only after the scope is understood; its budget angle complements rather than replaces sizing.

A qualified professional is required for roof work, battery interconnection, DC isolation and protection, mains integration and commissioning. PV conductors can remain energised in daylight, and batteries can deliver severe fault currents. Do not open enclosures or improvise a changeover connection.

6. Test the plan before asking for quotes

Try a practical training exercise: double only the fan hours from six to twelve. Fan energy becomes 1,440 Wh and total energy becomes 2,156 Wh, while simultaneous running power remains 272 W. Battery energy at the original assumptions becomes about 3.59 kWh. The inverter running target does not change. Explaining that distinction is better evidence of understanding than remembering a package size.

Keep an assumption register beside the quote: measured or estimated watts, runtime, DoD, efficiency, reserve, design month and expected charge source. If the system later disappoints, this register helps distinguish changed use from a design or equipment fault. Do not resolve a repeated overload by bypassing protection.

Frequently asked questions

Does a 5 kW inverter require 5 kW of panels?

No fixed match follows from the inverter output rating alone. Load energy, charging needs, array limits and site yield determine the PV proposal. Manufacturer limits can rule out an otherwise attractive arrangement.

Can I add a new battery to an old bank?

Do not assume compatibility. Chemistry, age, capacity, firmware, voltage and manufacturer restrictions matter. Ask the supplier and installer for a supported design rather than mixing batteries because the labels look similar.

Will this cover two days without sun?

The worked example budgets one specified backup period, not two sunless days. Longer autonomy requires a new energy and recharge plan, with practical limits on battery size and charging time.
