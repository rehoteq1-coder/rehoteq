AI Adoption in Africa: The Common Problems and the Solutions Writers Agree On

A summary of the problems and solutions the main publications on African AI adoption keep returning to, with sources.

By Rehoteq Editorial TeamLast updated: 28 October 2026
About the author
Rehoteq Editorial Team produces practical explainers for Nigerian households, schools and small organisations. Read our editorial policy.

The same problems, named again and again

Read the main publications on artificial intelligence in Africa side by side and a pattern appears: they do not disagree much. The most-cited open-access treatment, the Springer book Responsible AI in Africa, lists the recurring barriers as skills acquisition, the lack of a structured data ecosystem, ethics, government policy, insufficient infrastructure and network connectivity, uncertainty, and user attitude. Other studies name the same list in different clothes. The SME-focused work adds cost and access to funding for small businesses; the language-focused work adds linguistic complexity and unclear data ownership; MIT Technology Review reduces the field to two hard constraints, compute and data.

Two of these deserve emphasis because they sit underneath all the others. First, data: African users often run machine learning systems trained abroad on data that does not recognise substantial parts of the African population, so the results can be biased against the very people using them. Second, skills: AI expertise is scarce, demand for it outruns supply, and much of the talent trained on the continent works elsewhere. Infrastructure and policy matter, but a team with data and skills can often route around them; the reverse is not true.

The solutions the literature converges on

The proposed remedies repeat across sources almost as consistently as the problems.

  1. Training and localised education. Nearly every paper calls for AI skills programs designed for African contexts rather than imported curricula, from university partnerships to community bootcamps.
  2. Local data collection. The data-scarcity literature proposes structured community data-collection challenges that reward representative local datasets, the same impulse behind KenCorpus and MasakhaNER.
  3. Community-built corpora. For language AI specifically, the 2025 inclusive-NLP review synthesises Masakhane, Mozilla Common Voice and the Deep Learning Indaba as the working answer to linguistic exclusion, and argues language inclusion is both a technical and a cultural imperative.
  4. Funding and partnerships for small businesses. The SME literature recommends tailored funding for African SMEs adopting AI, public-private partnerships, and lower training costs, because most African businesses are small and their adoption decisions move the continent’s numbers.
  5. Policy clarity and data governance. Ethics chapters argue for national frameworks that make data rights, procurement and responsible use predictable, so builders can plan and citizens are protected.

What this means for a builder in Nigeria

For a team building today, the summary is practical. The problems are known and named, which removes the excuse of novelty; the solutions are also known, and none of them requires waiting for a national policy to land first. The community route, open corpora, shared baselines and modest, useful tools, is the path every one of these publications either documents already working or recommends next. Our three-part Indigenous AI series covers that route in depth, from the starting point and time, through culture and heritage, to integration and actualization.

Limits of this guide: it summarises the shared findings of a small set of publications, most published between 2022 and 2025. Findings differ by country and sector, and conditions change quickly. Read the sources directly and verify current conditions before making a business, research or policy decision from this page.
