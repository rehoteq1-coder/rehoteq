WhatsApp Phishing Protection in Nigeria

A Nigerian reader’s practical guide to recognising WhatsApp phishing, protecting accounts and responding calmly to suspicious messages.

By Rehoteq Editorial TeamLast updated: 1 September 2026
About the author
Rehoteq Editorial Team produces practical explainers for Nigerian households, schools and small organisations. Read our editorial policy.

WhatsApp messages can look convincing because they arrive from a known contact, use a familiar logo or create pressure around a delivery, bank alert, job offer, account code or urgent family request. The safest habit is to slow down before you tap, forward a code or send money. This guide covers practical account protection; it cannot confirm whether a particular message, person or payment is genuine.

Phishing is an attempt to make you disclose information, install something harmful or take an action you would not otherwise take. The message may ask you to “verify” an account, vote in a promotion, recover a suspended line, claim a benefit, or help a friend with a new number. A message being polite, local or urgent is not proof of authenticity.

Pause and check independently

Do not use the link, phone number or reply button inside a suspicious message to verify it. Instead, open the organisation’s official app or type its known website address yourself. For a person who appears to have a new number, call a number you already know or ask a question only that person can answer without revealing sensitive information. Be especially cautious when a request bypasses normal family or workplace approval.

Look for pressure, unusual wording, requests for a one-time code, a link that does not match the claimed organisation, and offers that require a payment or login immediately. These are warning signs, not a complete test. A well-written message can still be fraudulent, and a poorly written one can be from a real contact. Independent verification is more reliable than judging spelling alone.

Protect the WhatsApp account itself

  1. Use WhatsApp’s available account-security features, including two-step verification, and keep the associated email or recovery information secure.
  2. Never share an SMS, verification or two-step code with anyone who asks, including someone claiming to be support.
  3. Keep WhatsApp and the phone operating system updated from official stores.
  4. Review linked devices and remove sessions you do not recognise.
  5. Use a screen lock and do not leave an unlocked phone with sensitive chats open.
  6. Limit profile-photo, status and group-adding visibility using the app’s privacy settings where appropriate.

WhatsApp publishes security information through its official security page. App menus can change, so use the current in-app settings and official help materials rather than following screenshots from a forwarded message. Be wary of unofficial “WhatsApp upgrade” files, modified apps and tools that promise to reveal another person’s messages.

Keep payments and identity details separate from chat pressure

A chat should not be the only evidence for a payment. If a supplier changes bank details or a relative requests money, use an independently known channel to confirm the request. Do not send bank card details, PINs, passwords, national-identity information, one-time passwords or full document images merely because someone says a payment is pending. Genuine organisations may contact customers, but they do not need your secret authentication code to prove you own an account.

Worked hypothetical example: a business receives a WhatsApp message from a familiar-looking supplier account asking for a transfer to a “new company account” before delivery. Rather than reply in the chat, the business calls the supplier using the number saved from a prior invoice and follows its internal payment approval process. If the supplier cannot confirm, no transfer is made. This illustrates verification, not a claim that every account-change message is fraudulent.

If you clicked, replied or shared a code

Act promptly, but do not panic. If you entered a password into a suspicious site, change that password through the real service and change any other account using the same password. If you shared a financial credential or initiated a transfer, contact the relevant bank or payment provider through its official channel immediately. If you lose WhatsApp access, use the official app’s account recovery process and alert close contacts through another route so they do not trust messages from the compromised account.

Preserve useful evidence such as screenshots, the account name, time and link before blocking or reporting when safe to do so. Use WhatsApp’s in-app reporting and blocking tools for suspicious accounts. Nigeria’s NITDA CERRT is an official cybersecurity resource; its services page is linked below. Follow the reporting channels of the affected bank, platform or relevant authorities for the incident type.

Set family and workplace rules before trouble

Agree that no one sends a code or money after a WhatsApp-only request without a second check. Establish a verification phrase for close family if that is useful, but remember that a phrase is not a substitute for calling an independently known number. In a workplace, set who can approve payment-detail changes and ensure a staff member is not punished for pausing an unusual request.

Remember: a report or block may help reduce further contact, but it does not undo a transfer or remove a malicious file. Keep device security updated and seek prompt official support for an account or financial incident.

The goal is not to become suspicious of every message. It is to make high-risk actions—sharing codes, logging in through links and sending money—require an independent check.
