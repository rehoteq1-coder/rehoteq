CBT Readiness Checklist for Nigerian Schools

A staged readiness checklist for Nigerian schools planning internal computer-based tests without overpromising capacity or reliability.

By Rehoteq Editorial TeamLast updated: 1 September 2026
About the author
Rehoteq Editorial Team produces practical explainers for Nigerian households, schools and small organisations. Read our editorial policy.

Computer-based testing (CBT) can help a school practise digital exam routines and reduce repetitive marking for suitable question types. It can also fail noisily when power, devices, user accounts or invigilation have not been planned. This guide is for internal school assessments. It does not certify a centre for JAMB or any other examination body, and it does not state that a school’s practice platform matches an external examination format.

Readiness begins with the assessment purpose. Choose whether CBT will be a low-stakes practice, a class test, a mock examination or a term assessment. Keep the first use narrow. A timed multiple-choice quiz with a small group creates a useful learning opportunity; attempting an entire school’s high-stakes examination without a trial can create avoidable disputes.

Start with a capacity and risk check

Count functioning devices, not devices believed to be available. Record usable screens, keyboards or touch input, chargers, seating, ventilation and accessibility needs. Time how long it takes a learner to sign in and open a sample test. If 60 learners need 20 working devices, plan sessions, supervision and breaks rather than promising simultaneous access.

Check electrical supply and connectivity independently. A browser-based platform may need stable access throughout; a locally delivered test may still need power and a way to collect results. Identify backup power, safe cable routes and who has authority to pause a session if a fault occurs. Never overload sockets or string unsuitable extension leads across walkways merely to make an exam start on time.

Pre-test checklist

  1. Question quality: review every question, answer key, mark allocation and image before publishing.
  2. Candidate list: verify names and identifiers; create a tested process for a new or absent learner.
  3. Accounts: issue individual logins; never make candidates share an invigilator password.
  4. Devices: charge and test each device, input method, browser and display at least a day ahead.
  5. Timing: set the correct duration and a clear rule for late arrivals, pauses and disruptions.
  6. Invigilation: brief supervisors on seating, permitted materials, technical escalation and incident records.
  7. Contingency: decide in advance whether and how a candidate resumes after a device or power failure.

Teach the interface before testing the subject

Students should practise the mechanics at low stakes: selecting an answer, moving between questions, using any flag/review control, reading a timer and submitting deliberately. This is not “teaching to the test”; it separates digital navigation from subject knowledge. Give learners a short familiarisation activity and allow questions about the interface before the timed assessment starts.

Use accessible language in instructions. Explain where to seek help without raising a hand for every screen change. Consider candidates with visual, motor or learning needs and decide in advance what reasonable support the school can provide. Do not improvise accommodations during a pressured session; test them alongside the platform.

Protect fairness and records

A CBT score is only as reliable as the question setup and the session record. Limit who can edit questions and answers after review. Preserve the version used, candidate responses where appropriate, completion time information and a record of material incidents. If a device freezes, write down the learner, device, time and action taken. This protects both the learner and the school when a score is queried.

Worked hypothetical example: a class of 48 is scheduled in two sessions of 24 because 26 devices passed testing. A two-device reserve is kept charged. The same approved question bank is configured with an agreed random order, and the exam officer records the start and end of each session. A learner whose device fails after 12 minutes is moved to the reserve device under the pre-agreed resume rule. The example is a planning pattern, not an examination regulation.

After the session: review before publishing

Automatic marking does not remove the need for review. Check that the question key was correct, identify questions with technical display issues, reconcile the candidate list and review incidents before scores are released. Do not silently alter a result after release; use the school’s correction process and explain the change to the affected learner or parent as appropriate.

JAMB publishes its own information and tools for its examinations. Schools should direct candidates to JAMB’s official channels for JAMB-specific requirements rather than relying on copied social-media instructions. Internal CBT practice may build confidence, but it cannot predict an individual’s performance in an external examination.

Go/no-go rule: postpone or revert to a safe approved alternative if essential devices, power, accounts or supervision are not ready. Fairness is more important than forcing a digital test to proceed.

A successful first CBT is one in which every learner understands the process, technical problems are documented, and the school can explain how a score was produced.
