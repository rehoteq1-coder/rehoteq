An AI That Speaks Volumes About Us: Culture, Morals, Heritage and History

What it means for AI built for Nigerians to carry our languages, values, heritage and history, and how classrooms can start building it this term.

By Rehoteq Editorial TeamLast updated: 28 October 2026
About the author
Rehoteq Editorial Team produces practical explainers for Nigerian households, schools and small organisations. Read our editorial policy.

The question every Nigerian AI must answer

Before any model is trained or any app is shipped, an African AI faces one question: whose voice does it speak in? Not the accent alone. Whose proverbs does it reach for when explaining patience? Whose greeting conventions does it respect, the ones where age and title decide who speaks first? An AI that answers a Nigerian in fluent English while treating Yoruba, Hausa, Igbo, Tiv, Efik and Pidgin as an afterthought has already answered the question, and answered it wrongly. An AI that speaks volumes about our culture is not decoration on top of a foreign product. It is a different product, built from different materials.

Culture is data before it is software

What a model knows is what it has read, and most of what our communities know has never been typed. Proverbs are stored in the memories of grandmothers, not in corpora. Market negotiation, the half-English half-Yoruba of a Lagos bus stop, the tonal subtleties that change a word's meaning, none of it exists in usable digital form until someone deliberately records it. So the first act of building a culturally fluent AI is unglamorous: record the elders, transcribe the songs, label the speech, and keep licensing clean so the data stays African-owned. Whichever generation does the recording decides what the next generation's machines can say.

Morals: teaching the machine our values

Nigerian communities carry their ethics in stories long before anyone writes a policy document. The tortoise tale that punishes greed, the proverb that instructs a child to wash their hands before eating with elders, the expectation that wealth is confirmed by community service and not just displayed: these are moral reasoning, stored in narrative form. A serious Nigerian AI should be able to draw on that reasoning, and the classroom can start training it. When pupils use a model, the lesson should ask not only "was the answer correct?" but "was it fair?", "was it respectful?", "would this advice make sense in our town?" Those questions, asked from Primary 4 upward, produce adults who expect their technology to behave like a member of the community rather than a stranger with a microphone.

Heritage: a memory that outlives us

Heritage work is where AI becomes urgent rather than optional. Oral history does not survive neglect; it dies with its holders. The same speech-to-text tools that power voice assistants can transcribe an elder's account of a town's founding. The same image models that label products can help catalogue museum photographs and masquerade regalia. Translation systems trained on our languages keep dialects navigable for children who grew up in the diaspora. Every year of delay makes the archive thinner. Every recording made now is a primary source for a historian not yet born.

History: an AI that checks itself

History is also where AI fails most dangerously, because a language model can invent a plausible past with total confidence. A machine that hallucinates a fake king or a fabricated treaty does more damage than one that simply knows nothing, which is why the discipline of sourcing matters as much in African AI as in African journalism. The classroom exercise is simple and powerful: ask the model a question about Nigerian history, check its claim against a real book or archive, and record where it invented. Pupils who do this weekly learn the deepest lesson in computing, that a confident answer is not the same as a true one.

The builders are already showing how

None of this is hypothetical. The Masakhane community, founded in 2019 by Jade Abbott and Vukosi Marivate, has volunteers across the continent building datasets and translation models for African languages under a name that means "we build together". GhanaNLP turned its research into Khaya, a translation app for Ghanaian languages. In South Africa, Lelapa AI released Vulavula, a voice-to-text tool for under-served African languages, and InkubaLM, a multilingual small language model built for low-resource languages. The Deep Learning Indaba keeps supplying trained researchers. These teams demonstrate the essential argument: culture-carrying AI is built by people who treat their own languages as primary data, not as a market segment.

Start in the classroom, not in a ministry

The fastest way to get an AI that speaks volumes about us is to raise the children who will build it, and that work happens now, in primary and secondary schools. Pupils can train a small image model on local foods and fabrics, record a proverb collection as a class corpus, and fact-check a chatbot against a history textbook. The scheme of work we published alongside this article builds exactly these exercises into a weekly rhythm any Nigerian school can run. The technology will only speak for our culture if the people building it learned, early, that their own world is worth encoding.

Limits of this guide: it is an argument and a set of classroom starting points, not a technical implementation plan. Cultural data collection carries consent, privacy and licensing obligations that vary by community and jurisdiction, and oral-history recording should follow the guidance of the community and any relevant archive or museum.
