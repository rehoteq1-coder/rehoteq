Generator Changeover & Backup Power: What Safe Looks Like

An owner-level walkthrough of source isolation, essential loads, warning signs and the handover to demand from an electrical professional.

Rehoteq Editorial Team · Updated 23 September 2026 · Editorial policy

How to read this lesson. The worked scenarios are original, illustrative training examples, not documented customer jobs or claims of a completed inspection. No named practitioner review is claimed. Use the sources and ask a competent professional to check decisions for your situation.

A generator starting successfully does not prove that the building’s backup arrangement is safe. The important questions appear between the engine and the loads: can one source energise another, is the circuit protected, does the earthing arrangement remain effective, and can the person on duty operate it without guessing? This lesson turns those questions into a practical inspection brief, without providing terminal-by-terminal wiring instructions.

The setting is an illustrative school office in Nigeria. During outages it wants lights, a router, a laptop and a printer, but not the pump or air conditioner. The office manager is responsible for defining that need and organising a competent inspection. The manager is not responsible for opening the distribution board or deciding which conductors should be switched.

Professional work only. Inspection, source-transfer installation, neutral/earthing decisions, protection and commissioning require a competent, authorised electrical professional. This lesson is an owner’s briefing, not a wiring manual. Do not work on live equipment.

1. List every source, not just the generator

Write down grid supply, generator, inverter, battery-backed sockets and any solar equipment. An inverter can energise circuits when the generator is stopped; a solar system may still have live DC conductors when an AC switch is off. A label saying “main off” is not evidence that every source is isolated. Give the full list to the electrical professional before any work begins.

A safe transfer arrangement prevents unintended interconnection of sources. A properly designed break-before-make transfer is one familiar approach for ordinary standby service, but equipment selection and the permitted operating sequence depend on the installation. Synchronised parallel generation is specialist work, not a shortcut made by combining switches. A web diagram cannot decide your source arrangement.

Start with our short changeover safety guide. During the site visit, ask the professional to explain in plain language what prevents backfeed and what remains live after a normal shutdown. If the explanation depends on “remember never to touch those two switches together,” request a proper review of the interlocking and operating controls.

2. Agree the essential-load plan

For the training office, suppose lighting totals 100 W, a router uses 15 W and a laptop supply draws up to 65 W. Their assumed simultaneous load is 180 W. A 600 W printer adds a possible 780 W total while printing. This example does not select a generator: power factor, brief peaks, actual printer behaviour and the generator’s continuous rating are still unknown.

The useful outcome is a load policy. The office records which sockets and circuits are backed up, who may connect extra appliances, and which loads remain off. A laminated instruction at the desk is more useful than assuming everyone remembers a conversation. Separation of essential circuits, if needed, must be professionally designed and installed.

A pump or compressor can make a modest running total misleading. Do not test capacity by repeatedly resetting a breaker or trying a bigger fuse. Record appliance model numbers and ask how simultaneous starts are handled. If the site has sensitive ICT equipment, also discuss power quality and the UPS required for orderly shutdown; an automatic transfer is not necessarily interruption-free.

3. Understand the cable question without doing the wiring

The cable has two related jobs: carry current without unacceptable heating under its installation conditions, and deliver adequate voltage at the load. Size alone does not settle either. A cable in a hot, crowded conduit behaves differently from the same size in a suitable open reference arrangement. The warmest part of the route can be decisive.

Use the cable size learning calculator as a discussion aid. Its separate 3,000 W single-phase example gives 3,000 ÷ 230 = 13.04 A at power factor 1. With its disclosed reference table, 40°C ambient and 30 m route, the model finds a 4 mm² candidate after checking current and the chosen drop budget. That result is not a recommendation for the office, the generator feed or a different installation method.

Then use the voltage drop calculator to see why route length matters. At 20 A, 30 m one-way and 4 mm² copper, its warm-conductor approximation gives 7.11 V drop at power factor 1, about 3.09% of 230 V. Upstream losses are additional. Starting demand, poor joints and a weak source are outside that simple calculation.

The professional must still coordinate protective devices with the conductor, establish fault-disconnection performance and check the generator’s behaviour under faults. Generator and inverter fault currents may differ from grid fault currents. Never interpret a low calculated voltage drop as proof that fault protection will operate correctly.

4. Recognise stop-use warning signs

  • An improvised lead used to feed the building through a wall socket.
  • Exposed live parts, a missing cover, melted insulation, scorching or a burning smell.
  • Repeated trips, sparks, unusual heat, water ingress or a shock sensation from equipment.
  • Unlabelled controls, defeated interlocks, or an operating sequence known to only one absent person.
  • A generator running indoors, in a garage or near openings where exhaust can enter occupied rooms.

Do not touch suspect metalwork or open the panel to investigate. Keep people away and obtain qualified help. Isolate only through a known, accessible normal control if doing so does not put you near the hazard. In an electric-shock emergency, do not touch a casualty who may remain in contact with live electricity; contact emergency services and keep clear until the source is safely isolated.

The electrical safety guide helps identify these warning signs, but remote advice cannot certify the premises. A previous trouble-free month is not evidence that an improvised connection is acceptable.

5. Treat exhaust as a separate fatal hazard

Carbon monoxide cannot be reliably detected by smell. The US CDC advises operating portable generators outdoors, more than 20 feet, approximately 6 metres, from windows, doors and vents; never inside a home or garage even with doors open. That guidance is not a guarantee that any particular outdoor location is safe: neighbouring openings, wind, enclosures and the manufacturer’s instructions still matter.

Install suitable battery-powered or battery-backup CO detection following its instructions. Keep exhaust clear and do not solve rain exposure by enclosing the generator in a space that traps fumes. If the compound cannot support a safe location, obtain professional advice instead of accepting corridor operation as unavoidable.

Headache, dizziness, weakness, vomiting, chest pain or confusion during generator operation can be an emergency. Move to fresh air without entering a hazardous space to rescue someone and seek urgent medical assistance. Refuel only when the equipment is stopped and cooled according to its manual; keep fuel handling away from ignition sources.

6. Make handover testable

Request equipment identification, circuit labels, a simple source diagram prepared by the professional, commissioning and test records, operating instructions and a maintenance schedule. Ask who is authorised to operate the system and who may modify it. Keep a copy of the documents where the responsible staff can reach them during an outage, not only on a computer that has lost power.

Have the installer demonstrate normal use and explain responses to a failed start, a trip and a return of grid supply. This is supervised familiarisation, not a fault-creation exercise. Do not deliberately short equipment or disconnect protective conductors to “see if it works.” Confirm that labels can be read in poor light and that replacements can follow the same instructions.

The wiring cost estimator can help organise a budget conversation. Safety-critical survey work, testing, suitable transfer equipment and remedial work must remain in the scope even if the original request was merely “fit a changeover.” A cheaper quote that silently omits them is not an equivalent job.

Frequently asked questions

Is an automatic transfer switch always safer than a manual one?

Not automatically. Correct specification, installation, interlocking, protection, testing and maintenance matter for both. Automation does not cure a flawed source arrangement or unsafe generator location.

Should the neutral be switched?

That depends on the supply, earthing, generator and transfer design. It cannot be decided from a photograph or a universal online rule. An authorised electrical professional must assess the complete system.

Can the caretaker follow a wiring video?

No. Operating a commissioned system under clear instructions is different from installing or altering it. Work affecting mains, source transfer, earthing or protection requires a competent professional and the applicable inspection process.
