How to Do a Solar Load Audit in Nigeria

A careful, practical solar load-audit method for Nigerian homes and small businesses, with a clearly labelled worked example.

By Rehoteq Editorial TeamLast updated: 1 September 2026
About the author
Rehoteq Editorial Team produces practical explainers for Nigerian households, schools and small organisations. Read our editorial policy.

A solar load audit is a list of what you want to power, how much electricity each item uses and when it is used. It is the beginning of a design conversation, not a quotation or a promise that a particular system will run every appliance. In Nigeria, household supply patterns, heat, battery type, building wiring and future load changes make site-specific assessment especially important.

The most useful audit is honest about priorities. Separate essential loads—such as selected lights, refrigeration where appropriate, communications or a work device—from loads that are desirable but energy-intensive. Electric cookers, pressing irons, water heaters, pumping equipment and air conditioners may need a different design discussion. Avoid assuming that a panel or inverter rating printed in an advert answers the question.

Make an inventory from the actual premises

Walk through each room and write each appliance, its quantity, the wattage shown on its rating label and likely daily hours. A plug-in energy meter can help with suitable plug loads; for fixed wiring and larger equipment, involve a competent installer. Do not rely solely on a device name: two “fans” or “fridges” can have very different labels and operating patterns.

Record peak use separately from daily energy. A system may have enough stored energy in theory but still be unable to start a motor or support several loads at once. Note appliances that cycle, have motors, heat elements or chargers. Ask which loads must work at the same time and which can be scheduled. This reduces the temptation to choose equipment only by its largest printed number.

Calculate an initial energy estimate

The basic estimate is wattage multiplied by hours of use, expressed in watt-hours (Wh). Add the items to obtain a daily estimate. Treat this as a starting figure. Nameplate ratings may be maximum values; real use changes with settings, temperature, age and duty cycle. A designer also needs to allow for system losses, battery operating limits, sunlight conditions, cabling and an appropriate margin.

Worked hypothetical example: a household lists six 10 W LED lamps for five hours (300 Wh), two 45 W fans for six hours (540 Wh), a 65 W television for four hours (260 Wh), a 100 W decoder for four hours (400 Wh) and a 60 W laptop for three hours (180 Wh). The simple listed total is 1,680 Wh per day. It is not a final system size: it excludes losses, surge requirements, the refrigerator, battery limits and site conditions. It should be taken to a qualified installer as an input, not used to order equipment online.

Use a priority table

  1. Tier 1: loads required during an outage or overnight, with realistic hours.
  2. Tier 2: loads used when there is sufficient solar production or battery charge.
  3. Tier 3: high-energy loads normally left to grid, generator or a separately designed system.
  4. Future loads: planned freezer, pump, office equipment or air conditioner; label them as future, not current.
  5. Operating rules: agree what turns off first when energy is limited.

For a small business, repeat the inventory by operating period. A shop may use lighting and a point-of-sale device through the day but need little night backup. A clinic, cold-storage user or workshop can have higher consequences if power fails; that calls for an appropriately qualified site assessment, not a generic calculator result.

Inspect the site safely

Before discussing panel numbers, observe roof condition, shading, orientation, mounting space, cable route, distribution board, earthing arrangement and safe equipment location. Do not climb a roof, open a panel or alter a distribution board for an audit unless you are competent and authorised to do so. Batteries and inverters need suitable ventilation and protection from moisture, heat, damage and unauthorised access according to manufacturer instructions and the design.

Ask how the solar supply will relate to grid and generator power. Improvised backfeeding can endanger occupants and utility workers. A changeover or other interface must be selected and installed for the actual system by a competent professional. The Energy Commission of Nigeria and the Nigerian Electricity Regulatory Commission are relevant starting points for official energy-sector information; local requirements and installation details still need checking.

Questions to take to an installer

  • Which exact loads and simultaneous demand were used for the proposal?
  • What assumptions are made for solar resource, losses and battery usable capacity?
  • How will motor starting, overload protection, earthing and isolation be handled?
  • What is the equipment warranty, who supports it, and what maintenance is expected?
  • What happens when the battery state is low, and how are grid or generator sources isolated?
Safety and financial limit: this calculation guide cannot determine safe cable size, protective devices, roof structure, battery suitability or a justified project price. Do not use it to make electrical connections or commit to a purchase without a site-specific assessment.

A good audit makes assumptions visible. It enables an installer to challenge the right things and enables a customer to compare proposals on load coverage, safety provisions and operating rules—not just on a headline kVA figure.
